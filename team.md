## What I Bring to a Team

The best work I've ever done wasn't a single ticket. It was a team that wanted to show up for each other.

### Embedded with Engineering

At Auditude, I was an Escalation Engineer embedded with the engineering team. Our TSEs in New York escalated tickets to me, I took the first real swing at them, and I escalated to engineering when a problem needed their hands.

I sat in their meetings and worked alongside them every day. Part of what I brought was a support engineer's instinct for troubleshooting: scoping the problem, asking what changed, reading the evidence before guessing. I taught that to the engineers, and they taught me the product from the inside. Both sides got sharper.

I also sat in on sprint planning, and over time I became so integral to the team that 50% of the sprint budget was at my discretion. I could use it to ask questions, host troubleshooting sessions, or push a customer-impacting bug up the queue. That kind of trust isn't handed out. It's earned.

### Tokens of Appreciation

When I first joined, I had a lot of questions, and the engineers were generous with their time. I wanted them to know I noticed, so I started handing out stickers as a thank-you. They loved it.

Halloween was coming up, so next I gave everyone a bendy monster. Those monsters became the hallmark of the team. Eight months later, new hires were asking when they'd get theirs.

I kept it going. Little trinkets at the major holidays. Birthday gifts given from the whole group, so everyone was part of it. Nothing expensive, just cheap plastic toys from Oriental Trading. But each one was a steady signal that the people around me were seen and appreciated.

### The Duck Bash

At one point I flagged to our Director of Engineering that we had 60 open bugs that needed attention. He proposed a 24-hour bug bash, and I brought the prizes: rubber ducks, every one dressed differently. Firefighter, pirate, zombie, and so on.

I laid all 30 ducks out on a table and let the engineers parade past to pick their favorites. When the clock officially started, they literally ran back to their desks. Every completed fix earned the duck of your choice.

I ran out of ducks. They kept bashing anyway, because the clock was still ticking and they knew I'd get more.

48 of the 60 bugs were squashed in a single day.

### Lunch, Not Shop

There were 24 engineers on the team, and at least a dozen of them came to lunch with me regularly. We never talked shop. We got to know each other as human beings, and that built what the military calls unit cohesion.

After Adobe acquired us and we moved to headquarters, we kept going to lunch together in the company cafeteria. At first there wasn't a table big enough for our group, so we pushed a few together. People looked at us like we were a little strange. A few minutes later they saw us laughing and joking with each other, and they decided they wanted that, too. The cafeteria staff eventually rearranged the layout to add more big tables, and before long three or four groups were filling them every day.

The appreciation habit came with us, too. Our new floor had walled offices, and mine was crammed with so much furniture it felt claustrophobic. I asked the facilities team to take most of it away, and I gave them stickers as a thank-you. They were genuinely touched. One of them loved his so much he put it on his badge. From then on, whenever Auditude needed anything, facilities put us at the top of their list.

I always laughed to myself about it. Tiny 48-person Auditude showed 13,000-person Adobe how to be a little more human.

### Just in Case

Six months after the acquisition, we had our first major release. The entire C-suite was watching, and everyone was nervous.

Releases usually happened after 5 pm. Normally two engineers and our IT guy stayed behind to do the rollout, and everyone else went home.

Not that day. I walked down the hall at 5 pm and everyone was still at their desks. I asked a couple of people why they were still there. The answer was the same: "Just in case." They wanted to be ready to jump in if anything went wrong.

The release deployed without a hitch. The team dove in with tests to verify it, and everything worked perfectly.

Afterward, over a dozen of us went to the after-hours dinner in the smaller cafeteria. We took up nearly the whole room, and we laughed and joked until well past 8 pm.

That's what unit cohesion looks like when it counts.

### Why It Matters

Small things aren't small. People who feel appreciated go above and beyond, and that team did.

I'm good at solving hard problems. But I'm proudest of making the people around me better at solving them, and making it a place they want to be. A team that trusts each other closes tickets faster than any one person ever could.

I still look for ways to do this wherever I land.
